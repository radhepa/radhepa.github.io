Writing a circuit simulator from scratch

How a netlist becomes a matrix, how a diode gets solved, and how a capacitor is stepped through time, in about 1,250 lines of C.

Goal

I use circuit simulators in class and on projects, but I treated them as a black box. I wanted to know what actually happens between typing a netlist and getting a waveform, so I wrote a small SPICE-style simulator in C with no libraries, and checked it against ngspice.

How it works

Every circuit becomes A x = b. Kirchhoff's current law at each node gives one equation per node. The unknowns are the node voltages, plus one extra unknown for every voltage source and inductor, because their current can't be written from the voltages alone. Each element adds ("stamps") a few numbers into the matrix. A resistor between nodes a and b adds +1/R on the diagonal at a and b and −1/R off the diagonal. Solving the system gives every voltage at once.

Capacitors become resistors, one step at a time. A capacitor follows i = C dv/dt. Over one time step h, the trapezoid area rule from calculus turns that into a conductance 2C/h in parallel with a current source that carries the previous step's voltage and current. So at every time point the whole circuit is just resistors and sources again. This is the stamp in the code:

/* i_new = geq * v_new - ieq */
double geq = (pt->trap ? 2.0 : 1.0) * e->value / pt->h;
double ieq = geq * e->v_prev + (pt->trap ? e->i_prev : 0.0);
stamp_g(w, a, b, geq);
stamp_i(w, a, b, ieq);

Diodes need Newton-Raphson. The diode current is exponential in voltage, so the system isn't linear any more. The fix is to replace the diode with its tangent line at the current guess, solve, re-linearize at the new answer, and repeat until the answer stops moving.

What needed extra care

Getting the basic loop right was the easy part. Making it robust took most of the work, and every fix turned out to be something real SPICE also does:

  • Ringing at sharp edges. The trapezoidal rule oscillates right after a discontinuity, like the edge of a PULSE source. One backward-Euler step after each corner damps it.
  • Exponential overflow. A bad Newton guess can push the diode voltage high enough that eV/nVt overflows. Junction-voltage limiting caps how far one iteration can move.
  • Missing corners. If a time step jumps over the corner of a pulse, the waveform is wrong. Breakpoints make the stepper land exactly on every corner.
  • No DC starting point. When Newton can't find the operating point, gmin stepping starts with large conductances to ground (an easy, nearly linear problem) and removes them gradually.

Note: Halving the time step cut the error by 4× with the trapezoidal rule and by 2× with backward Euler. The measured slopes were 1.92 and 0.98, matching the theory (error ∝ h² vs h).
